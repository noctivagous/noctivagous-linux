The desktop that is also a home server

DIYers — the Hackaday and XDA crowd — increasingly run real infrastructure at home: media, photos, passwords, home automation, on a repurposed mini-PC or an old laptop. The stack has converged, the pain is operational, and no mainstream desktop treats hosting as a first-class job. This report maps who they are, what they run, where it hurts, what existing systems teach us, and the concrete system and UI moves Noctivagous can make.

Compiled 26 Nov 2026 from community surveys (selfh.st 2025 & 2026, an independent 850+ respondent survey), project documentation, and Hackaday / XDA reporting. Figures carry their source and hedges inline; the Open questions section lists what could not be verified.

§ Executive summary

This is not a niche that wants a prettier desktop. It is a large, maturing, production-like home-infrastructure audience — and its frustrations are specific enough to design against.

The opportunity

No mainstream desktop does the thing these users cobble together from Proxmox + Cockpit + CasaOS/YunoHost + Tailscale + Uptime Kuma: a desktop that is also a first-class, inspectable home server — a services centre, one-click installs that stay transparent to the underlying Compose file, private-by-default remote access, explicit consent before anything is internet-facing, pre-update snapshots with one-click rollback, tested backups, and power/headless modes for always-on use. That gap, not another launcher, is the product.

Motivation is control, not just thrift

In an independent 850+ respondent 2025 survey, the primary motivation was Fun/Hobby (31.3%), then Privacy (16.7%), Independence from third parties (16.6%) and Data control (12.5%). Linuxiac's reporting of selfh.st 2025 likewise found that aside from the hobby aspect, most respondents named privacy — and 81% run Linux.

Sources: selfhosted-survey-2025.deployn.de · Linuxiac

The pain is operational, not installational

XDA's year-in review names it plainly: non-automatic, sometimes-breaking updates; remote access as “its own project” (CGNAT, TLS, DNS); storage silently filling with logs, snapshots and caches; projects disappearing; and becoming unpaid IT for family who now depend on your server.

Source: XDA — operational headaches no one warns you about (read in full)

Lock-in is the unforgivable sin — proven twice in 2025

Plex paywalled remote playback of your own files (April 2025) and kept raising its lifetime pass; Synology restricted its 2025 Plus NAS line to its own branded drives, then reversed the policy in DSM 7.3 (8 Oct 2025) after sales fell. Samsung removing OEM unlocking in One UI 8 is the Android equivalent. A forced account, paywalled remote access, a hardware whitelist or a non-open app-store gate will be read by this audience as the same move.

Sources: How-To Geek on Plex pricing · Consumer Rights Wiki on Synology

Confidence

High for the stack, motivations and precedents — multiple surveys and project/community sources agree. Medium for exact 2026 percentages: selfh.st's own 2026 results page is chart images, so those figures come from secondary analysis of it. XDA and Hackaday are editorial/project communities, not random samples — they show what this audience does and celebrates, not population prevalence.

1 Who they are and what they actually do

Four overlapping sub-groups — most real people are two or three at once. The XDA writer whose $150 mini-PC runs 12 containers is also the person flashing a ROM on a phone the OEM abandoned.

Sub-group A

Homelabbers

Run Proxmox clusters, VMs/LXC, firewalls and NAS; often learning skills they use at work. Motivation: learning, fun, control.

But scale is modest: in selfh.st 2026, clusters were a minority fringe (736 of 6,221) against 3,841 on consumer hardware — most people run one discreet or repurposed box.

Sub-group B

Privacy / cost / control self-hosters

Replace Google Photos, Drive, Netflix, 1Password and Google Home with their own services, for themselves and their family. Motivation: privacy, data control, independence, subscription fatigue.

Docker's own survey summary lists the same drivers: privacy and data sovereignty, escaping subscription fatigue, avoiding lock-in, and the joy of customisation.

Source: Docker blog

Sub-group C

Android modders — the XDA core

Unlock bootloaders, root (Magisk/KernelSU), flash LineageOS, GrapheneOS, crDroid; de-Google with microG; keep old phones alive past OEM support. Motivation: control of their own hardware, privacy, longevity.

The workflow is unchanged for a decade: Developer Options → OEM unlocking + USB debugging → adb reboot bootloader → fastboot flashing unlock → custom recovery → ROM. A desktop that makes ADB/fastboot painful — drivers, udev, stale platform-tools — breaks this group's core workflow directly.

Evidence: XDA forums are organised per-device into ROMs/Kernels/Recoveries; LineageOS builds for the 2014 OnePlus One were still updated in June 2025. XDA thread

Sub-group D

Hardware hackers / makers — the Hackaday core

ESP32, Arduino, RP2040, 3D printing, soldering, salvaged panels and thin clients, custom PCBs, cyberdecks. Motivation: building itself; repurposing; learning.

The Hackaday front-page pattern: a Dell Wyse thin client becomes an OpenWrt router after defeating a BIOS lock; salvaged laptop panels become monitors; a VTech toy becomes an Arch Linux “PinkPad” laptop.

Sources: Hackaday — thin client projects · Hackaday — laptop hacks

The hardware reality

  • The server is usually repurposed. An old desktop, an old laptop, a used mini-PC (OptiPlex Micro / ThinkCentre Tiny / ProDesk Mini), a NUC, a NAS, or a Pi. XDA argues in 2026 that old devices now beat a new Raspberry Pi on price/performance after Pi price hikes — except on power. An old laptop adds a built-in “UPS” (its battery) plus a screen and keyboard for rescue. XDA · XDA
  • The Pi is the on-ramp, with known limits. Raspberry Pi is the top SBC (94% among SBC users in the independent survey) for Pi-hole, Home Assistant and single-purpose servers. Its limits are openly discussed: SD-card wear, CPU/IO ceiling when stacking services, rising kit price. At the extreme low-power end, Hackaday.io's Solar Pi runs a Pi Zero W on Alpine in diskless RAM mode at 1–2 W idle, for months unattended. Hackaday.io — Solar Pi
  • NAS is near-universal. About two-thirds of selfh.st respondents deploy one; in the independent survey, custom-built is the top NAS type (50%) — ahead of Synology/QNAP appliances. XDA's old-PC-to-NAS guide pairs an old gaming rig and IronWolf drives with TrueNAS Community Edition, and warns an old desktop pulls 40–110 W. XDA
  • Electronics work is mundane and specific. Arduino/ESP32/RP2040 flashing via USB-serial, esptool, Arduino IDE/PlatformIO. The needs are unglamorous: serial permission without sudo, stable device names, nothing stealing the adapter, working toolchains (§3).
Who they are not

Not enterprise sysadmins: in selfh.st 2025 discussion, nearly all respondents self-host for personal use (only ~8 of 4,000+ indicated enterprise). Not non-technical consumers either: they read release notes, edit YAML, and accept being “sysadmin, network engineer, and on-call all at once” — but increasingly resent it. That resentment is the design opening.

Patterns they expect

  • Install: official Docker image + official Compose file, or one-click from a catalogue that shows that Compose file. Data in a named, visible directory they can copy. No curl-pipe-to-bash as the only path.
  • Update: pull + up -d, pinned or at least visible image tags (not blind latest for stateful apps), release notes before updating, and a backup/snapshot taken first. Watchtower-style blind auto-update is controversial for stateful services.
  • Backup: 3-2-1, with Borg/Restic as the respected engines; app-level DB dump + files, in that order, with a copy off the box. Zerobyte (Restic + web UI) being spotlighted by selfh.st in Nov 2025 is a signal of unmet demand for backup UX. selfh.st weekly
  • Rebuildability is the new uptime: mature users keep Compose/Ansible in Git, secrets outside the repo, and judge a setup by “can I rebuild it from scratch on a fresh Debian install?” Base OS reflects this: Debian (2,954) and Ubuntu (2,584) dominate 2026 responses, well ahead of Arch/Fedora/Alpine.
  • Panel loyalty is fragile: Portainer fell from 66% to 33% share among panel users in one year (selfh.st 2026), after it announced the end of Community Edition feature development — users fled to Dockge, Dockhand, Komodo, Arcane. This audience punishes rug-pulls instantly and migrates in months.

3 Where it hurts

Two layers of friction: self-hosting itself, and what mainstream Linux desktops add on top. Both are documented in the community's own words.

Self-hosting itself

Updates break things #1 complaint

“Self-hosted projects ship changes on their own schedule, so the chances that something breaks when you update is really high… Immich recently moved the server's internal port from 3001 to 2283, which would break your setup unless you made edits to your Compose file and the reverse proxy.”— XDA, after a year of self-hosting FOSS tools

Immich's own releases repeat the pattern at scale: v1.133.0 required a database vector-extension migration (pgvecto.rs → VectorChord), a DB backup first, matching mobile/server versions, and warned “you should not downgrade below 1.133.0.” TrueNAS rewrote its entire apps stack (k3s → Docker Compose in 24.10) with a hard 1 June 2025 auto-migration cutoff.

Sources: Immich v1.133.0 release notes · TrueNAS migration research

Remote access is a second project #1 complaint

Choosing between port-forward + reverse proxy, overlay VPN, VPS relay or tunnel — “none of those are obviously correct for a beginner, and it compounds if you're behind CGNAT… you've now taken on the burden of TLS certs, DNS records, and other configurations that accumulate” (XDA).

CGNAT: a router port-forward rule does nothing when the ISP shares one public IPv4 among customers (WAN in 100.64.0.0/10); detection alone requires comparing router WAN vs public IP. Dynamic IP/DDNS, ISP-blocked 80/443 and double NAT are separate failure modes in the same chain: DNS → router → firewall → proxy → app; if one link fails, everything fails. TLS: Caddy automates Let's Encrypt; nginx+certbot and Traefik label-config fail silently — a renewal failure “breaks TLS silently until certificate expires,” and a missing Caddy /data volume loses ACME keys and hits Let's Encrypt rate limits for up to 7 days.

The workaround became a dependency: Tailscale and Cloudflare Tunnel solve CGNAT by outbound connection, which is why they won — but self-hosters then worry about the hosted control plane, default connection logging and outages (selfh.st noted Cloudflare/GitHub/Tailscale outages “impacting self-hosted infrastructure across the globe” in a single week, Nov 2025). Headscale, NetBird and Pangolin exist purely to self-host that control plane.

Sources: SSD Nodes — CGNAT · The DNS→proxy→app failure chain · Caddy in Docker · Tailscale vs WireGuard · Project Homelab — remote access

Backup/restore is where data is actually lost High

Immich's automatic DB dumps write to the same disk as the photos and contain no photos — “a dead drive takes out your photos and the backup of their metadata in one go.” A USENIX study of 994 self-hosters plus 41 interviews found security approached “in an unstructured way,” with “vague catch-all concepts such as firewalls and backups” dominating, without reflection on the threat mitigated. Restores are rarely tested until they fail.

Sources: Self-hosting Immich without losing a photo · USENIX Security '23 study

Failures are silent; storage fills invisibly High

Services fail silently — Immich's web UI loads while its ML container OOM-crashes repeatedly; phone uploads stop and “the server never noticed anything was missing.” Hence Uptime Kuma's ubiquity, and the mature-lab pattern of alerting when a backup doesn't run, not just when a service is down. And: “Logs are the classic killer, but snapshots, local backups, preview files, caches, indexes… can pile up fast” (XDA). No mainstream desktop warns a hoster that a disk is 90% full of Docker overlays and thumbnails.

Power, noise, heat — and responsibility High

An old desktop NAS idles at 40–110 W; a rack server ~150 W and loud; a Pi at 1–5 W; a mini-PC/NUC is the accepted compromise. Always-on also means power cuts: no UPS means an unreachable password vault and photo library exactly when needed. And once family depend on your Jellyfin, Immich and Vaultwarden, downtime is not deferrable — “you're now responsible for getting things back online” (XDA). The community norm has also flipped on exposure: from “forward 80/443 to Traefik” to “open no ports; VPN/tunnel + SSO in front” — exposing admin UIs (Portainer, Cockpit :9090, Proxmox :8006) to the internet is warned against everywhere.

Projects disappear, get acquired, change terms High

Booklore (10k+ stars) vanished overnight — repo, Discord, site — and users discovered it via a 404 on docker pull; it survived only because it could be forked (as Grimmory). Portainer CE, Plex, Synology drives, the Gitea→Forgejo flight and Arduino/Qualcomm (below) are the same pattern.

What mainstream Linux desktops add

No server mode

Desktop distros assume a person at a screen who sleeps and suspends the machine, uses NetworkManager Wi-Fi, and reboots for updates. Homelab use needs the opposite defaults: no suspend, lid-close = keep serving, wired/static IP, wake-on-LAN, services surviving logout, headless rescue via SSH/web. Users currently bolt this on with systemctl mask sleep.target, logind.conf edits and luck.

Permissions and file sharing

Samba sharing from GNOME/KDE still routinely requires editing smb.conf, installing plugins, or fixing fstab/CIFS “permission denied” errors. Containers add a second permission universe (PUID/PGID, rootful Docker socket, bind-mount ownership) that no desktop UI explains. A service that can't read the media folder is the most common first-week failure.

Hardware-hacking friction — dialout/udev hell

Arduino, ESP32, 3D printers, SDR and ADB all present as /dev/ttyUSB0/ttyACM0 owned by root:dialout (Debian) or root:uucp (Arch) — the group name differs by distro, adding yourself requires re-login, and forum answers still include chmod 666 and hand-written udev rules with single-vs-double = typos that silently fail. Flatpak sandboxing compounds it: a sandboxed IDE or flashing tool cannot see the serial device at all. ModemManager and brltty can also steal the adapter.

Sources: Arch forum thread · USB serial issues roundup

Trust violations are read as betrayal, not inconvenience

Plex: free remote playback removed April 2025; the lifetime pass went $119.99 → $249.99 → $749.99 (July 2026). Samsung/Android: One UI 8 (July 2025) removed the OEM-unlocking toggle in every region; without unlock there is “no custom recovery, no ROM, no root.” Xiaomi and OnePlus added server-side permission, waiting periods and quotas; Google's Play Integrity API lets banking apps refuse rooted/ROM devices. Arduino/Qualcomm (Nov 2025): new terms perceived as claiming rights over user creations, banning reverse engineering and expanding data collection triggered immediate backlash; Arduino had to publish “anything that was open, stays open.” The lesson is about trust velocity: this audience reads terms changes within hours. Driver/firmware tinkering adds its own friction — Jellyfin transcoding needs Intel Quick Sync with the render group set correctly; Wi-Fi/BT firmware, NVIDIA, and HAT/GPIO support on SBCs are all distro-specific.

Sources: How-To Geek · The Custom Rom — Samsung · Beebom — custom ROMs 2025 · Hackster — Arduino clarifies

Case study in trust velocity — the Plex lifetime pass

Price of Plex's lifetime pass as it paywalled remote playback of users' own media. The response was migration to Jellyfin, now the clear #1 media server in the surveys above.

Source: How-To Geek. Free remote playback was removed in April 2025.

4 Precedents — what existing systems teach

Every pattern in §5 already exists somewhere. So does every failure mode. The table below is the evidence base: what works, and what drew criticism.

SystemPattern that worksWhat drew criticism / limits

UI patterns that demonstrably work — synthesis

  1. Tile catalogue, transparent underneath

    One-click install and the visible underlying Compose file or package.

  2. One dashboard, two jobs

    A single home view that is both launchpad (Homepage-style) and health view (Uptime Kuma-style).

  3. Web admin and native UI

    Reachable at hostname.local / a stable LAN name, plus a native desktop UI for the machine in front of you — users use both.

  4. Logs and metrics in place

    Per service, where the service lives — not in a separate observability stack.

  5. HTTPS and private remote access as distinct, labelled choices

    One-click HTTPS; one-click private remote access (VPN) — never conflated.

  6. Snapshot before update, one-click rollback

    The Proxmox / TrueNAS / Home Assistant OS pattern.

  7. Community stores as additional registries

    Not a single gatekeeper — StartOS/Umbrel community stores, Unraid Community Apps, HACS for Home Assistant.

5 Design implications for Noctivagous

Proven demand first — each item traces to evidence in §1–§4. Speculative items and proven anti-patterns follow, kept separate on purpose.

The flagship interaction: exposure as a permission

Every service is LAN-only by default. Making anything internet-facing is an explicit, per-service ask, modelled like a phone permission — showing the port, the domain, whether auth/SSO is in front, and a plain-language risk line. Revocable in one place: a single “What's exposed?” screen. This answers the community's #1 safety failure (unstructured exposure, per the USENIX study) without paternalism. Proven demand — build these

  1. Two host modes, first-class in Settings

    Host on this machine: turning the desktop into an always-on host flips a coherent profile — inhibit suspend/sleep, lid-close keeps serving, SSH + web admin on, static/DHCP-reserved IP prompt, server firewall profile, update policy = notify-not-reboot. Host on another machine at home: the desktop as console for a headless box in a cupboard — discover it, provision it (Pi Imager-style pre-seeding of user/Wi-Fi/SSH keys), manage its services remotely. This matches how people actually grow: desktop first, second box second.

  2. Services / Containers Centre — native, with a web twin

    Docker Compose as the native format: import any compose.yaml from URL/Git/file; export everything back out — no lock-in. Catalogue entries are recipes wrapping the upstream Compose, showing image, ports, volumes, and a diff before update. Start/stop/restart, per-service logs, resource use, “open web UI,” .env editing with secrets masked. Stateless-over-the-system architecture à la Cockpit: the GUI edits the real Compose/systemd state, so terminal and GUI never diverge.

  3. The exposure permission model

    As sketched above: LAN-only default, per-service ask before anything is internet-facing, one “What's exposed?” screen. Maps to the distro's existing principle that every exposure is an explicit ask under the security manager.

  4. Remote-access wizard that handles the ugly chain

    Detect CGNAT (WAN vs public IP), dynamic IP, blocked ports; then offer, in order: Tailscale/WireGuard VPN (private, recommended), self-hosted control plane (Headscale/NetBird), tunnel (Cloudflare/Pangolin), or classic port-forward + DDNS + reverse proxy + Let's Encrypt via Caddy-style automation. One flow provisions DDNS, proxy route and certificate together — and tests it from outside the LAN before declaring success. YunoHost's diagnosis tool is the precedent.

  5. Update safety

    Pre-update snapshot (btrfs/ZFS/timeshift-style for the OS; DB dump + volume snapshot per service), release-notes and breaking-change flagging (parse upstream “BREAKING CHANGE / Action Required” — Immich-style), staged updates (update one service, health-check, proceed), and one-click rollback — including an honest warning where a data migration can't be downgraded. Never auto-update stateful services silently; auto-apply OS security updates in a scheduled window.

  6. Backup that is tested, not just configured

    Guided 3-2-1 setup (local disk + off-box copy), Borg/Restic engine, per-service “what actually needs backing up” (DB + files, in order — not the vendor's misleading default), encryption, and scheduled restore drills with a green/red “last restore test” badge. Alert when a backup doesn't run.

  7. Health and alerting built in

    Service-down, disk >85%, container OOM/restart-loop, certificate-expiring, backup-missed and update-available notifications — to the desktop and a phone-reachable channel (ntfy/Gotify/email). A Homepage/Uptime-Kuma-style dashboard as the default “Home” view for host mode, with widgets for other machines and services in the house — a natural fit for the desktop's dashboard-widget window style.

  8. LAN discovery and sharing without the Samba ritual

    mDNS/Bonjour advertisement (jellyfin.local, nas.local), one-click folder share (SMB + a browser link) with user/password creation inline, NFS/SFTP toggles, and a “devices & services on my network” browser. Sharing works from the file manager's right-click, with the firewall opened for that share only and shown on the exposure screen.

  9. Power and always-on controls

    Idle-watts estimate, per-service wake behaviour, scheduled sleep/wake, WoL send/receive, UPS (NUT) status widget, laptop-battery-as-UPS mode (warn + graceful service shutdown at low battery), fan/noise profile, and “turn the screen off but keep serving.” Old hardware is the target: the whole host stack must idle light — DietPi and Cockpit's socket-activation are the bar: near-zero when nobody is looking.

  10. Hardware-hacking support out of the box

    A “Developer / Maker” settings page that (a) offers one-click serial access — adds the user to the correct group, whatever this distro calls it, without re-login pain; (b) ships curated udev rules for Arduino/ESP32/ESP8266, STM32, RP2040, CH340/CP210x/FTDI adapters, 3D printers, SDR, and Android (adb/fastboot with correct permissions, current platform-tools); (c) offers esptool, Arduino IDE/PlatformIO, OpenOCD, picocom — unsandboxed or correctly device-passed-through when installed as Flatpaks; (d) enables GPIO/I2C/SPI on SBC builds; (e) provides a serial monitor and a “what is this USB device?” view (lsusb/udev properties in human form). Plus an Android companion view: detect a plugged phone, offer ADB pairing, scrcpy, file transfer, flashing-tool status.

  11. Openness guarantees, stated as policy

    No forced account (an account, if any, is for optional sync only; the server works fully without it). No telemetry by default (opt-in, with a local log of what would be sent). Sideloading and custom catalogue registries/repos as a supported, documented path. Every catalogue app removable, its data exportable. OS and catalogue licences shown per app — flag source-available (Umbrel's PolyForm) as such; never present it as open source. Publish these as commitments: this audience will check after the first controversial update.

  12. Terminal and scripting openness

    First-class terminal; a systemd unit viewer/editor in Settings (unit status, journal, enable/disable — Cockpit's services page, natively); a cron/timer UI; and everything the Services Centre does also available as CLI/API, so setups stay in Git and rebuildable.

Speculative — validate before building

  • Local-AI centre (Ollama/Open WebUI one-click, GPU detection) — demand is real in self-hosting generally (2026 growth), but whether users want it from their desktop distro vs a container is unproven.
  • Household user management / family SSO (authentik-style) bundled — YunoHost proves it for multi-user servers; single-admin homes may not need it. Offer as an optional recipe, not core identity.
  • Phone companion app for the distro's dashboard — Uptime Kuma/HA apps suggest appetite; building and maintaining mobile apps is a large commitment.
  • One-click public publishing (blogs/sites/fediverse) — YunoHost territory; most homelab demand is private/family access first.
  • Energy-cost estimation per service (£/$ per month) — plausible delight, no direct survey evidence.

Anti-recommendations — proven to backfire

  • Forced account, or a cloud relay as the only remote path (Plex).
  • Paywalling remote access or transcoding of the user's own media.
  • Hardware/drive whitelists (Synology).
  • Removing a developer/unlock feature in an update (Samsung).
  • A single curated store with no sideload or Compose import (Umbrel/CasaOS limits).
  • Blind auto-updates of stateful services (Immich/TrueNAS breakage).
  • A web admin that maintains its own divergent config database (the Webmin anti-pattern, vs Cockpit).
